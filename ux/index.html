Design review & UX thinking

Pretty isn't the same as good.

I'm a visual designer who notices when a screen looks finished and still lets people down. Then I write down exactly why.

This page is the short version of how I think about usability, hierarchy, and accessibility. It walks through one product concept, SORE THUMB, and then does something a little uncomfortable: I review my own AI-assisted mockups and point out where they fail the design they're supposed to show.

How I review a design

Five questions, in order.

I start with what the person is trying to do, not with how it looks. Looks come last because they're the easiest thing to be fooled by.

1. The flow

Can someone get from start to done without extra steps, dead ends, or guessing what happens next?

2. The hierarchy

Is the most important thing the most visible thing? Or is the one action that matters buried?

3. Accessibility

Does it work without color? Is the contrast high enough, the text big enough, and are the targets easy to hit?

4. Consistency

Do the same things look and behave the same everywhere? Do the labels match from screen to screen?

5. The copy

Do the words say what's actually happening? Unclear wording is a design problem too.

Where this comes from: explaining technology to customers at Best Buy taught me to notice where people get confused. Cleanroom finishing at Greene Tweed taught me to check work against a defined standard, not a vibe. A software engineering bootcamp at Flatiron School taught me how screens actually get built.

Case study · SORE THUMB · Self-directed product concept

Keep your place.

Attention moves. The task can stay visible.

The problem

Most task tools assume you work in a straight line: make a list, start at the top, check things off. A lot of people don't work that way. I'm one of them. With ADHD, I task jump. I start cleaning one corner of my room, spot something that belongs somewhere else, and now I'm on a different corner. To me, those little piles are already a kind of organization.

That leads to three problems with the usual tools:

  1. Out of sight, out of mind. A task buried in an app might as well not exist.
  2. Linear lists punish switching. If you jump between tasks, a top-to-bottom list makes you feel behind even when you're getting things done.
  3. Finished work disappears. Check a box and it's gone. Then at the end of the day it feels like you did "nothing."

Design goal

A system that keeps tasks visible, lets you switch between them without losing your place, and shows you that you made progress.

My role

Product concept, task-flow rules, interaction decisions, accessibility constraints, brand direction, and creative direction for AI-assisted mockups.

Status

A concept. It hasn't been prototyped physically or tested with users yet. I'm saying that up front because a mockup isn't proof that something works.

The flow, and why each stage works the way it does

Saved for laterKeep the idea without making it today's job.

Nothing here is due. My morning brain dump mixes real tasks with stray thoughts, and giving those thoughts a home keeps "Up next" honest.

Up nextPull a task in when you mean to start it.

Moving a note into this column is a small, deliberate decision. That's the point.

In motionActive work. More than one task can live here.

Doing one thing at a time is good advice, but people who switch tasks ignore systems that forbid it. The risk is that the column overflows, which testing has to check.

Made itRecognize what you finished, then clear it.

A little "Hey, you did this today" moment. Keeping every note forever would just make one more pile to manage.

Why only four stages? Every stage you add is one more decision about where a note belongs. I wanted sorting a task to take seconds, not a planning session.

Where it could fail, and what I designed against

Color can't be the only way to show status. Some people can't tell certain colors apart, and color fades in bad lighting. Each stage needs a written label and a fixed spot on the board. Color is a bonus, not the only cue.

A missing note doesn't mean the task is done. If a note falls off, the board shouldn't quietly make it look finished. Only "Made it" means complete. Where a note sits is what counts.

The notes have to be usable. If they're too small to write a real task on, or you can't read them from a few feet away, the board stops being useful.

Travel can't scramble the board. If notes shift inside a bag, you lose your place, which is the one thing this product promises not to do.

A "stuck tasks" corner can't turn into a shame pile. I've thought about a space for tasks that just aren't moving, where you break them down instead of avoiding them. It's still an open idea, not part of the design, because I haven't figured out how to keep it from becoming a wall of failures.

Tradeoffs

  • Physical over digital, for now. An app could send reminders, but it hides tasks behind a screen, and hidden tasks are the problem I started with. An app is out of scope and hasn't been built.
  • Flexible over strict. Allowing more than one active task makes the board forgiving, but it relies on people keeping it tidy. I'd rather design something people keep using than a strict system they drop after a week.
  • Recognition over a full record. "Made it" gives you a moment of credit, not a log of every task ever. A full history would be a different product.
More about my process: the brain dump behind the board

A little "Hey, you did this today."

You know when someone asks what you did yesterday and you can barely remember what you had for breakfast? Sometimes I feel like I've done "nothing" all day, and then I can't remember enough to prove myself wrong. That's part of why finished tasks get a place too. But I don't want to keep every note forever. That feels like a bit much.

A reminder of the reminder

I remembered a Visible ad before I even knew what the product was. That made me wonder whether SORE THUMB's colors and thumbtack shape could work the same way: a sticker somewhere you pass every day, or a gritty texture on a phone sticker you'd notice by touch. These are questions I want to explore, not benefits I can promise.

Design review · My own mockups

Where my mockups get it wrong.

I made these with AI tools under my direction. They look polished. Several of them also quietly break the rules I just laid out.

This is the part I care about most. A model can make a slick image of a product and still miss how the product is supposed to work. Here's what I caught when I reviewed my own SORE THUMB mockups against the design.

Flow · Consistency

A stage is missing, and another one changed its name.

What's wrong
The design has four stages. This board shows three. "Saved for later" got pushed off to a separate notepad called "Out of My Head." The board also says "Next Up" while the design says "Up next."
Why it matters to the user
If the board doesn't match the system, people won't know whether a loose idea goes on the board or on the pad. Two names for the same stage make people wonder whether they're two different things.
What I'd change
Put all four stages on the board, or decide on purpose that the pad is the "Saved for later" stage and label it that way. Pick one name for each stage and use it everywhere.

What it gets right: the column labels are written out and underlined in color, so status doesn't depend on color alone. "Feels too big? Try one smaller step." is a nice nudge right where people get stuck.

Clear status

Some finished tasks are checked off. Some aren't.

What's wrong
In the "Made It" column, "Morning walk," "Declutter closet," and "Write thank you notes" have checkmarks. "Send invoices" and "Call dentist" don't.
Why it matters to the user
It adds a second signal for "done" that nobody explained. Now someone has to wonder whether an unchecked note in "Made It" is really finished. That's exactly the confusion the board is supposed to remove.
What I'd change
One signal: if it's in "Made It," it's done. Leave the checkmarks out, or make them a personal habit and not part of the system.
Hierarchy · Overload

The mockup shows the failure as if it's normal.

What's wrong
"In Motion" runs across both pages and holds about sixteen tasks. "Next Up" has three and "Made It" has four. The board also splits one stage across a fold.
Why it matters to the user
Overflow in "In Motion" is the biggest risk I named in the design, and the mockup makes it look like the goal. Sixteen active tasks isn't keeping your place. It's a wall. Splitting a stage across the fold also makes it read like two different sections.
What I'd change
Keep each stage on one surface. Show a realistic amount of active work. Then test whether the column needs a soft limit, like a visible "this is getting full" line, instead of a hard cap.
Consistency · Legibility

Each format reorganizes the board.

What's wrong
The book shows "In Motion" twice, once on each page. The wall board, book, and easel each put the stages in a different place. The book and easel tiles are much narrower than the 3 × 3 inch notes on the wall board, and the handwriting on them is tiny. The easel's tagline was also copied from the book ("A small book for a bigger day").
Why it matters to the user
Part of the promise is that the same task can move with you. If every format arranges the stages differently, people have to learn the layout all over again, and moving notes between formats gets harder. Small tiles make it hard to write a real task and hard to read it at a glance.
What I'd change
Use one fixed layout for every format: four stages in the same order and the same positions. Set a minimum tile size based on how much people actually write, then test whether it's readable from about arm's length.

Why include this? Because a polished image is the easiest place for a bad decision to hide, including my own. All mockups were made with AI tools under my creative direction. No physical prototype or user testing has been done yet.
